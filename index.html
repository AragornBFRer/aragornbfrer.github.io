Research

Machine learning now produces predictions and hypotheses in large numbers, but knowing which ones to trust, and how to act on them, is still hard. I develop statistical methods for assessing predictions and discoveries from modern machine-learning systems, including flexible and black-box models, with guarantees that hold under weak modeling assumptions.

  • When to trust a prediction. Conformal inference and uncertainty quantification: distribution-free prediction sets, principled abstention, and coverage that holds uniformly across heterogeneous populations rather than only on average.
  • Which discoveries are real. Data-driven selection makes findings look stronger than they are. Selective inference and multiple testing restore valid error control when many candidate features, models, or hypotheses are screened and only the most promising are reported.
  • What happens when we act. Causal inference: estimating the effects of interventions from experimental and observational data, and assessing whether those effects carry over to new populations and time periods.
  • Agentic scientific discovery. AI agents that propose hypotheses, design experiments, and interpret results face all three questions at once. When hypotheses are cheap to generate, the bottleneck shifts to validating them rigorously.
  • Evaluating AI systems. Judging a model by the statistical quality of its findings, such as whether they come with valid error control and whether the model has the power to detect real effects. Evaluations of this kind can also guide the training of better models.

I also enjoy the engineering side of research, particularly building tools and workflows that make statistical ideas useful in scientific and applied settings. More broadly, I am interested in turning research ideas into tools with real-world value. I am always glad to hear from researchers and practitioners working on related problems.
